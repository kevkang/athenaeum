Athenaeum
A research traceability tool for working scientists. AI does the archiving. You do the science.
The problem
Scientists spend 3–8 hours a week reading literature, and the tools meant to help them are pulling in the
wrong direction. Google Scholar is stagnating and structurally unstable — a free service Google has no
strategic commitment to maintain — yet it remains the de facto archivist of scientific research. The new
generation of AI research tools (Elicit, Consensus, and others) has raised hundreds of millions of dollars
optimizing a single paradigm: ask a question, get a synthesized answer with citations. The design goal
is speed-to-answer.
This model is fundamentally mismatched to how science actually works. Real research is a sustained
engagement with a field over months and years, not a sequence of stateless questions. "Consensus meters"
reduce nuanced evidence to vote counts. Hallucinated and misread citations are polluting the literature. Every
major tool is closed source, non-reproducible, and built for consumer-scale growth rather than scholarly trust.
(Not only do I think these are bad models for science, but I am also skeptical it will be profitable for the
companies pushing it.)
The underlying infrastructure to do better has quietly become available. OpenAlex now indexes 271 million
works under a CC0 license. Preprint servers host millions of papers in full text. Open annotation standards
exist. What's missing is a product built on this foundation that treats scientists as scientists rather than
passive consumers and regurgitators of AI output.
The project
"I propose to build an open-source AI research companion for working scientists." If you're looking for that,
you're in the wrong place.
There is no shortage of AI research companions that synthesize information and output summaries and
consensus statements. Athenaeum is not a research companion — it is a research traceability tool.
The product is built around sustained projects scoped to a researcher's active question. The project
accumulates a persistent context — papers, the scientist's own datasets, prior reasoning — and gets smarter
about that specific work over time. This component is necessary, but not innovative.
This is the important part: every AI-generated claim is anchored to specific sentences in specific papers,
quoted verbatim, verified by an independent checking pass. All reasoning is auditable — query
expansion is shown and editable, and retrieval, sentence extraction, signal detection, and citation verification
are exposed as a step-by-step reasoning trace on every synthesis.
The unit of work is the brick: a cemented assertion that rests on sentence-level evidence from specific
papers. Each citation carrying an explicit role (theory, method, opposing direction, stakes) and an exact
passage accumulates annotations, and builds on other bricks to form an argument scaffolding. A brick moves
from draft to cemented only when the scientist decides it should. Synthesis itself is inverted: the AI reports
what it found, N sources support this, M report the opposite sign, others bear on scale and stakes, and the
scientist writes the synthesis and logs an evaluation (agree / partial / disagree, with reasoning) against
each extracted passage. AI does what AI is good at: making connections. The user evaluates those
connections in a structured citation → annotation → connection → cementation loop.
Annotations, open questions, and discussions are first-class objects that accumulate into a personal
knowledge graph — papers linked by citation and co-citation, the scientist's annotations and questions
overlaid, opposing-direction edges flagged, and "ghost" papers (cited in the literature but not yet in the
project) surfaced for retrieval. Made public, the graph becomes a community corpus that enables independent
tracing and auditing of any evidence base. The entire project exports as a portable .ath framework file: a
human-readable, version-controllable YAML record of every brick, supporting passage, and builds-on relation,
with a full provenance trail (retrieval corpus, model and independent checker model, citation-verification rate,
logged evaluations). It compiles directly into a scientific paper attachment and can be dropped into any other
Athenaeum to reproduce the structure exactly and build on top of it. CC-BY-4.0.
The strategic wedge is not "better synthesis" but a different product category. Where incumbents optimize for
speed-to-answer, we optimize for depth-of-understanding. Where they are closed, we are open source: open
prompts, open evaluations, a public commitment to the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI),
and a citation-verification rate published openly every month, targeting a measured <1% hallucinatedcitation rate rather than a claimed one. Where they are structurally incompatible with a community layer
(because it conflicts with a subscription business model), we build community contribution as the long-term
moat.
A second phase integrates structured peer review and, eventually, platform-native publishing on top of the
research tool. Because the .ath framework is already a citation-grade manuscript substrate, the publishing
layer emerges from an already-engaged community rather than fighting the usual cold-start adoption problem:
utility wins first.
An opinionated framework
This is an opinionated project. I am a working scientist who sees a fundamental disconnect between how
science is done (properly) and how AI is being juiced to intervene. I am also a devout AI skeptic. I have seen
baseless claims and grift abound in this industry. Silicon Valley chants "AI is a tool" while building platforms
that maximize cognitive offloading of the user for speed and dependence. The philosophy of this project is
simple at its core:
The landscape and the wedge into the field
1. The future world crafted by scientists and engineers is ultimately inhabited by humans, not computers.
Therefore, it is humans who need to be responsible for assessing claims — not abstracting away
intellectual engagement in favor of expediency.
2. AI is powerful for parsing large volumes and presenting them coherently. In this framework, AI is the
archivist who searches the library; the user is the scholar who interprets.
The market has consolidated around a single product paradigm that experienced researchers find
unsatisfying. No major tool is open source, and the reform-minded scientific community does not trust closed
commercial AI for serious work. Open bibliographic infrastructure (OpenAlex, funded with $7.5M from Arcadia
in 2024) has removed the main technical dependency that previously favored incumbents. Foundation
funding for open scientific infrastructure is at a historic high following cOAlition S, the U.S. OSTP Nelson
memo, and European institutional mandates. Our commitment to POSI, to nonprofit governance, and to open
source is structural rather than rhetorical — the legal form prevents the main failure mode of successful open
infrastructure, which is acquisition by commercial publishers.
